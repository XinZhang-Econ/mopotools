# Quantitative Easing: Japan

**Summary**: The Bank of Japan pioneered quantitative easing in 2001-2006, then launched much larger "Quantitative and Qualitative Monetary Easing" (QQE) programs from 2013 onwards, eventually adopting yield curve control in 2016. Total BoJ holdings exceeded ¥500 trillion JPY (>90% of GDP). Despite massive asset purchases, Japan has struggled to achieve sustained 2% inflation.

**Research classification**: Empirical

**Sources**: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018-unconventional-monetary-policies-in-the-euro-area-japan-and-the-united-kingd.md, four-stories-of-quantitative-easing-pdf.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

## Background

Japan's experience with unconventional monetary policy predates the Global Financial Crisis. Following the collapse of Japan's asset bubble in the early 1990s, the economy entered a prolonged period of low growth and deflation. The Bank of Japan cut rates to zero in 1999 and launched its first QE program in 2001. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

When the GFC hit, Japan's GDP contracted by approximately 9% - worse than the US, UK, or euro area - partly due to the collapse in export demand.

## Historical Context: QE 2001-2006

**Period**: March 2001 - March 2006
**Purchases**: ¥35 trillion JPY (approximately $300 billion USD, ~7% of GDP)

**Outcome**: Mixed. The BoJ exited QE and raised rates in 2006 as the economy improved, but inflation remained near zero. Critics argue the BoJ:
- Was not committed to sustaining inflation
- Prematurely tightened policy
- Failed to anchor inflation expectations at a positive level

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

This experience shaped perceptions that the BoJ lacked commitment to reflation, creating headwinds for later programs.

## Post-Crisis Response (2008-2012)

### Initial Response

The BoJ's initial response was relatively modest:
- Marginal rate cut from 50 to 10 basis points
- Liquidity support for banks
- Small asset purchase program (¥5 trillion JPY, later ¥20 trillion JPY, ~4% of GDP)

### Forward Guidance (2010-2012)

**October 2010**: BoJ announced intention to keep rates low until "price stability is in sight," if no major financial risk materialized.

**February 2012**: Reiterated commitment to use asset purchases until inflation reaches the "1 percent goal."

**Effectiveness**: Limited. Deflationary expectations remained entrenched. Markets doubted the BoJ's commitment given its track record. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Abenomics Era (2013-Present)

### Political Context

Prime Minister Shinzo Abe's election in December 2012 transformed BoJ policy. "Abenomics" consisted of three arrows:
1. Aggressive monetary easing
2. Fiscal stimulus
3. Structural reforms

The political backing gave the BoJ credibility to pursue unprecedented stimulus.

### 2% Inflation Target (January 2013)

The BoJ adopted an explicit 2% inflation target - a significant departure from its previous "1% goal." This shift in framework itself had measurable effects on inflation expectations. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### QQE1 (April 2013)

**Announced**: April 4, 2013
**Annual purchases**: ¥50 trillion JPY JGBs (~10% of GDP/year), ¥1 trillion JPY ETFs, ¥30 billion JPY J-REITs
**Target**: Achieve 2% inflation "at the earliest possible time"

**Key features**:
- Open-ended commitment
- Purchases of risky assets (ETFs, REITs)
- Focus on increasing monetary base

### QQE2 (October 2014)

**Announced**: October 31, 2014
**Expanded purchases**: ¥80 trillion JPY JGBs (~16% of GDP/year), ¥3 trillion JPY ETFs, ¥90 billion JPY J-REITs per year

**Context**: Inflation had fallen back toward zero after a temporary increase, partly due to oil price declines.

### Negative Interest Rate Policy (January 2016)

**Announced**: January 29, 2016
**Rate**: -0.1% on marginal excess reserves

**Tiered system**: Only a portion of reserves subject to negative rates, limiting direct costs to banks.

### Yield Curve Control (September 2016)

**Announced**: September 21, 2016
**Framework**:
- Short-term rate target: -0.1%
- 10-year JGB yield target: approximately 0%

**Innovation**: Rather than targeting a quantity of purchases, the BoJ commits to whatever purchases are necessary to maintain the yield target.

**Rationale**: Address concerns that flat yield curve was harming bank profitability.

### Inflation Overshooting Commitment

The BoJ committed to expanding the monetary base until CPI inflation exceeds 2% on a sustained basis - aiming to convince markets that the target is symmetric and the BoJ will tolerate above-target inflation.

## Macroeconomic Effects

### Effects on Yields

| Study | Program | 10-Year Yield Impact |
|-------|---------|---------------------|
| Lam (2011) | Oct 2010 announcement | -10 bps |
| Arai (2017) | QQE1 | -14 bps |
| Hausman & Wieland (2014) | QQE1 | -11 bps |

Effects were smaller than in US/UK, partly because yields were already very low. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Effects on GDP and Inflation

| Study | Program | GDP Impact | Inflation Impact |
|-------|---------|------------|------------------|
| De Michelis & Iacoviello (2016) | 2% target | - | +0.8% |
| Hausman & Wieland (2014) | QQE1 | +1% | - |
| Kan et al. (2016) | QQE1 | +0.6-4.2% | +0.3-1.5% |

The wide ranges reflect uncertainty in identification. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Effects on Exchange Rate

QQE was associated with substantial yen depreciation, though much of this occurred in late 2012 when Abe's election and policy intentions became clear, prior to formal announcement. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Effects on Stock Prices

QQE1 was associated with increased stock prices. BoJ purchases of ETFs provided direct support to equity markets - a unique feature among major central banks.

## Why Has Japan Struggled?

Despite unprecedented stimulus, Japan has consistently undershot its 2% inflation target. Several factors explain this:

### Entrenched Deflationary Expectations

Decades of near-zero inflation created a "deflationary mindset" among firms and households. Wage-setting and price-setting behavior adapted to expect minimal inflation. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Forward Guidance Ineffectiveness at ZLB

When an economy is expected to remain at zero rates indefinitely, promises to keep rates low "until inflation rises" have limited impact on current behavior. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Credibility Deficit

The BoJ's 2006 exit from QE - prematurely, critics argue - damaged credibility. Markets remained skeptical that the BoJ would sustain stimulus long enough to achieve 2% inflation.

### Flat Yield Curve

With the yield curve already near zero across maturities, QE had limited room to reduce yields further.

### Demographics

Japan's aging and shrinking population creates structural headwinds for inflation.

## Lessons for Other Countries

Japan's experience suggests:

1. **Credibility is crucial**: QE is less effective when central bank commitment to reflation is doubted
2. **Early action matters**: Allowing deflation to become entrenched makes escape difficult
3. **Coordination helps**: Political backing (Abenomics) enhanced policy credibility
4. **Limits exist**: Even massive QE may be insufficient once deflationary expectations are anchored

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)
