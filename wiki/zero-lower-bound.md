# Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)

**Summary**: The zero lower bound is the constraint preventing central banks from cutting nominal interest rates significantly below zero, because agents can hold physical cash earning 0%. This constraint motivated the development of unconventional monetary policy tools including QE, forward guidance, and negative interest rates.

**Research classification**: Both (theoretical framework with empirical applications)

**Sources**: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018-unconventional-monetary-policies-in-the-euro-area-japan-and-the-united-kingd.md, bernanke20120831a-pdf.md, riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

## Overview

The zero lower bound (ZLB) refers to the constraint on conventional monetary policy arising from the option to hold physical currency. Since cash pays a nominal return of zero, nominal interest rates cannot fall much below zero without triggering a shift into cash holdings. This creates a floor on how far central banks can cut rates.

The ZLB became a binding constraint for major central banks following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, motivating the development of [[quantitative-easing]], [[forward-guidance]], and [[negative-interest-rates]].

## Theoretical Foundation

### The Cash Arbitrage

In standard models, nominal interest rates cannot go below zero because:

1. Agents can always hold physical currency
2. Currency pays a nominal return of zero
3. If deposit rates fell below zero, agents would withdraw deposits and hold cash
4. This arbitrage sets a floor at zero

### Why Zero?

The bound is at zero (rather than some other number) because:
- Cash is a liability of the central bank paying 0% interest
- It serves as a perfect substitute for reserves at the zero rate
- No storage costs in simplified models

## Effective Lower Bound (ELB)

In practice, the lower bound is slightly negative, not exactly zero. This "effective lower bound" (ELB) exists because holding cash involves costs:

1. **Storage costs**: Vault space, security, insurance
2. **Transaction costs**: Cash is less convenient than electronic payments
3. **Theft risk**: Cash can be stolen
4. **Large denomination limits**: Hard to convert large sums to cash

### How Negative Can Rates Go?

| Central Bank | Lowest Rate | Notes |
|--------------|-------------|-------|
| Swiss National Bank | -0.75% | Deepest negative rate |
| Danmarks Nationalbank | -0.75% | Tied with SNB |
| ECB | -0.50% | Deposit facility rate |
| Swedish Riksbank | -0.50% | February 2015 - December 2019 |
| Bank of Japan | -0.10% | Three-tier system limits exposure |

The practical ELB appears to be somewhere between -0.5% and -1% for most economies. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Implications for Monetary Policy

### The ZLB Problem

When the economy requires stimulus but rates are already at zero:

1. Conventional rate cuts are unavailable
2. Real interest rates may remain too high
3. Deflationary pressures can emerge
4. Traditional monetary policy becomes ineffective

This was the situation facing major central banks in 2008-2009 and again during the Covid-19 pandemic.

### Unconventional Policy Responses

The ZLB motivated development of new tools:

| Tool | How It Works Around ZLB |
|------|------------------------|
| [[quantitative-easing]] | Lowers long-term rates even when short rates at zero |
| [[forward-guidance]] | Commits to keeping rates low, reduces expected future rates |
| [[negative-interest-rates]] | Pushes slightly below zero, exploiting ELB |

## The Reversal Rate

### Concept

At some point, further rate cuts may become contractionary rather than expansionary. This theoretical "reversal rate" (Brunnermeier and Koby, 2018) arises because:

1. Bank profitability declines as rates go negative
2. Banks may reduce lending rather than pass through negative rates
3. Credit supply contracts, offsetting demand stimulus

### Evidence from Sweden

Eggertsson et al. (2024) found that pass-through to Swedish lending rates declined when the Riksbank cut below -0.25%, suggesting diminishing effectiveness:

- Above -0.25%: Strong pass-through to lending rates
- Below -0.25%: Weakened transmission, possibly related to bank equity values

This provides empirical support for reversal rate concerns. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Historical Episodes

### Japan (1990s-2000s)

Japan hit the ZLB in 1999 and remained near zero for most of the following two decades:
- First major economy to face binding ZLB
- Led to development of QE and later QQE
- Deflationary expectations became entrenched

### Global Financial Crisis (2008-2009)

Major central banks hit the ZLB almost simultaneously:
- Fed: December 2008 (0-0.25%)
- BoE: March 2009 (0.5%)
- ECB: Approached zero by 2014

All turned to unconventional policies as conventional ammunition was exhausted.

### Covid-19 (2020)

Central banks that had begun normalizing quickly returned to the ZLB:
- Fed: March 2020 (0-0.25%)
- BoE: March 2020 (0.1%)
- Riksbank: Already at 0% since December 2019

## Policy Design Considerations

### Avoiding the ZLB

Some economists have proposed structural changes to reduce ZLB frequency:

1. **Higher inflation targets**: 3-4% target gives more room to cut
2. **Price level targeting**: Commits to making up past misses
3. **Nominal GDP targeting**: Automatic stabilization
4. **Abolishing cash**: Removes arbitrage constraint

None have been adopted by major central banks.

### Operating at the ZLB

Given the ZLB, central banks have learned:

1. **Act early**: Don't wait until reaching zero to deploy unconventional tools
2. **Communicate clearly**: [[Forward-guidance]] is essential
3. **Use multiple tools**: Combine QE, guidance, and negative rates
4. **Accept trade-offs**: Unconventional policies have costs and risks

## The Swedish Experience

Sweden's Riksbank provides a case study of ZLB policy:

**2010-2011**: Raised rates despite below-target inflation (financial stability concerns)

**2015-2019**: At ZLB, deployed:
- Negative rates (down to -0.50%)
- Government bond purchases
- Forward guidance

**Outcome**: Inflation returned to target by 2018-2019, but at cost of balance sheet expansion that later required capital injection. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Related Concepts

### Liquidity Trap

A related concept where monetary expansion fails to stimulate spending because:
- Rates are already at zero
- Demand for money is perfectly elastic
- Keynesian "pushing on a string"

QE was designed partly to address this by working through channels other than the short rate.

### Secular Stagnation

Some economists argue the ZLB will bind more frequently due to:
- Declining natural rate of interest (r*)
- Aging demographics
- Productivity slowdown
- Global savings glut

If true, unconventional policies may become conventional.
