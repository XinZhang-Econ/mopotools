# Negative Interest Rate Policy (NIRP)

**Summary**: Negative interest rate policy involves setting the central bank's policy rate below zero, charging banks for holding excess reserves. Implemented by the ECB, Bank of Japan, and several other central banks to provide additional stimulus when rates are already at zero.

**Research classification**: Both (theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence)

**Sources**: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018-unconventional-monetary-policies-in-the-euro-area-japan-and-the-united-kingd.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

---

## How NIRP Works

Central banks charge, rather than pay, interest on the reserves that commercial banks hold at the central bank. The expectation is that banks will:

1. Reduce excess reserves by increasing lending
2. Purchase other financial assets
3. Pass negative rates through to depositors (partially)

This should reduce lending rates, increase credit supply, and boost asset prices across financial markets. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## The Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)

Traditional theory held that nominal rates cannot go below zero because agents would shift to holding cash (which pays zero). In practice, the "effective lower bound" (ELB) is modestly negative because:

- Storing cash involves transaction costs and theft risk
- Banks need deposit services regardless of rate
- Corporate deposits are harder to convert to cash

The practical ELB appears to be somewhere between -0.5% and -1%. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Tiering Systems

To limit costs to banks, several central banks use tiered systems:

**ECB**: Exempts required reserves; applies negative rate to excess reserves

**Bank of Japan**: Three-tier system
- Positive rate tier (basic balance)
- Zero rate tier (macro add-on)
- Negative rate tier (policy-rate balance)

Only marginal reserves are charged the negative rate, reducing the direct cost to banks. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Transmission Mechanisms

### Interest Rate Channel
Negative policy rates shift the entire yield curve down, reducing borrowing costs for firms and households.

### Portfolio Rebalancing
Banks facing negative returns on reserves may purchase other assets, pushing up prices and lowering yields.

### Exchange Rate Channel
Lower rates reduce currency attractiveness, potentially causing depreciation and boosting exports.

## Concerns and Side Effects

### Bank Profitability

Banks appear reluctant to pass negative rates through to retail depositors, fearing deposit flight. This compresses net interest margins and harms profitability.

However:
- Direct costs of negative reserves are small relative to bank balance sheets
- Banks have partly offset by increasing fee income
- Capital gains on bond holdings provide offsetting benefits

Evidence suggests large banks have generally coped, but smaller regional banks (especially in Japan) face more pressure. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Cash Hoarding

At sufficiently negative rates, agents may shift to cash. This hasn't materialized at current levels, but limits how far rates can go.

### Public Perception

Negative rates are perceived as "unfair" by some depositors and face political opposition. This constrains central bank flexibility.

### Reversal Rate Risk

At some point, further rate cuts may become contractionary if they harm bank intermediation capacity enough to reduce credit supply. This theoretical "reversal rate" is debated.

## Evidence on Effectiveness

### Euro Area

After the ECB's June 2014 NIRP announcement and subsequent cuts:
- Yield curves shifted down, especially short end
- German yields went negative out to 10 years
- Lending rates declined
- Euro depreciated approximately 30% vs USD (2013-2017)

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Japan

After January 2016 NIRP announcement:
- 10-year JGB yields fell below zero
- Deposit and lending rates declined
- Impact on GDP and inflation hard to identify
- Regional bank profitability concerns increased

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Sweden

Sweden's Riksbank was one of the first central banks to implement negative rates (February 2015, just after the ECB):

**Timeline**:
- February 2015: -0.10%
- July 2015: -0.35%
- February 2016: -0.50% (lowest)
- December 2019: Return to 0%

**Effectiveness**:
- Strong pass-through to money market rates and government debt yields
- Pass-through to lending rates declined at deeper negative levels

**Key finding**: Eggertsson et al (2024) find that pass-through to lending rates declined below -0.25%, related to impact on bank equity values. This suggests the July 2015 and February 2016 cuts may have been ineffective through standard demand channels. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

This is consistent with theoretical work on the "reversal rate" (Abadi, Brunnermeier & Koby 2023) - the point below which further cuts become counterproductive. Sweden may have approached this bound. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

See [[qe-sweden]] for full details.

## Interaction with QE

NIRP and [[quantitative-easing]] are complementary:

1. **QE pushes down long end of curve**; NIRP pushes down short end
2. **NIRP increases incentive to rebalance** away from reserves toward other assets
3. **Combined effect** is a flatter, lower yield curve

The ECB and BoJ used both tools simultaneously. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)
