# Quantitative Easing

**Summary**: Quantitative easing (QE) is a form of unconventional monetary policy in which a central bank purchases longer-term securities to increase the money supply and lower long-term interest rates when conventional policy rates are at or near zero.

**Research classification**: Both (theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence)

**Sources**: bernanke20120831a-pdf.md, four-stories-of-quantitative-easing-pdf.md, qe-the-story-so-far-pdf.md, dell-ariccia-et-al-2018-unconventional-monetary-policies-in-the-euro-area-japan-and-the-united-kingd.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

## Definition

Quantitative easing involves the large-scale purchase of securities by the central bank, typically financed by the creation of central bank reserves. The central bank acquires long-term government bonds (an asset) and credits commercial banks' reserve accounts (a liability). (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

Former Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke famously noted that QE "works in practice, but it doesn't work in theory" - referring to the theoretical prediction that in frictionless markets, central bank asset purchases should have no effect on yields. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Why QE?

When policy rates hit the **zero lower bound** (ZLB), conventional monetary policy becomes constrained. Central banks cannot lower short-term rates further, so they turn to unconventional tools:

1. **Quantitative easing** - Asset purchases to lower long-term rates
2. **[[forward-guidance]]** - Communication about future policy path
3. **[[negative-interest-rates]]** - Pushing rates slightly below zero

## Transmission Mechanisms

QE works through several [[transmission-channels]]:

### Portfolio Balance Channel
When the central bank purchases long-term bonds, it removes duration risk from private portfolios. Investors who sell bonds to the central bank must rebalance into other assets (corporate bonds, equities, real estate), pushing up prices and lowering yields across asset classes. (source: bernanke20120831a-pdf.md)

### Signaling Channel
Large-scale bond purchases signal the central bank's commitment to keeping policy rates low for an extended period. This affects expectations of future short rates, which in turn influences long-term yields. (source: four-stories-of-quantitative-easing-pdf.md)

### Liquidity Channel
QE can improve market functioning during periods of stress by providing liquidity and reducing liquidity premia. (source: qe-the-story-so-far-pdf.md)

### Bank Lending Channel
In the UK model, QE creates broad money (deposits) when the central bank purchases gilts from non-bank financial institutions. This excess money in the system should eventually flow into spending and lending. (source: the-impact-of-qe-on-the-uk-economy-some-supportive-monetarist-arithmetic-pdf.md)

## Macroeconomic Effects

The empirical literature finds that QE has:

- **[[effects-on-yields]]**: Reduced long-term government bond yields by 40-120 basis points per program
- **[[effects-on-gdp]]**: Raised output by 1-3% depending on program and country
- **[[effects-on-inflation]]**: Increased inflation by 0.5-2 percentage points

However, there is significant uncertainty in these estimates. See [[research-debates]] for discussion of methodological issues and the gap between central bank and academic findings.

## Conditions for Effectiveness

Research suggests QE is more effective when:

1. **Financial markets are distressed** - Market segmentation increases the impact of removing duration risk (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

2. **Central bank credibility is high** - Markets must believe the central bank is committed to sustained accommodation (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

3. **Deflationary expectations are not entrenched** - Japan's experience shows QE is less effective when zero inflation is deeply embedded in expectations (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Potential Side Effects

Critics have raised concerns about:

- **Bank profitability**: Flattening the yield curve compresses net interest margins
- **Risk-taking**: "Search for yield" may push investors into excessively risky assets
- **Asset bubbles**: Inflated asset prices may become detached from fundamentals
- **Central bank independence**: Expanding into fiscal-adjacent territory invites political scrutiny
- **Exit challenges**: Unwinding large balance sheets may disrupt markets

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)
