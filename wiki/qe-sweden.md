# QE in Sweden (Riksbank)

**Summary**: Sweden's Riksbank deployed negative interest rates (down to -0.50%) and quantitative easing from 2015-2022, first to combat persistent inflation undershooting, then to address the Covid-19 crisis. The programs successfully brought inflation back to target but resulted in significant balance sheet losses when rates rose, requiring a SEK 25 billion capital injection in 2024.

**Research classification**: Both (comprehensive empirical evaluation with theoretical frameworks)

**Sources**: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

---

## Overview

Sweden provides a distinctive case study in unconventional monetary policy. As a small, open economy with a floating exchange rate, the Riksbank's experience highlights the role of the exchange rate channel in transmitting QE effects. The 2026 Ravn-Wilkins evaluation commissioned by the Swedish Parliament offers the most comprehensive assessment of Sweden's unconventional monetary policy experience. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Timeline of Unconventional Policies

### Pre-QE Context (2010-2014)

In 2010-2011, the Riksbank raised the policy rate from 0.25% to 2% despite below-target inflation, partly due to financial stability concerns about household indebtedness. This tightening:
- Contributed to persistent inflation undershooting
- Drew criticism from Deputy Governor Lars Svensson
- Created the backdrop for subsequent unconventional policies

By late 2014, the policy rate had been cut back to zero. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Phase 1: Pre-Covid Period (2015-2019)

| Date | Action | Details |
|------|--------|---------|
| February 2015 | First negative rate | Policy rate cut to -0.10% |
| February 2015 | QE begins | SEK 10 billion government bond purchases |
| July 2015 | Rate cut | To -0.35% |
| February 2016 | Lowest rate | Policy rate reaches -0.50% |
| 2015-2017 | QE expansion | Total holdings reach SEK 330 billion |
| December 2018 | First hike | Policy rate increased to -0.25% |
| December 2019 | Exit negative rates | Policy rate returns to 0% |

**Objectives**: Combat persistent inflation undershooting, reanchor inflation expectations, prevent krona appreciation as ECB eased.

**Peak government bond holdings**: ~SEK 380 billion (~35% of outstanding government bonds) (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Phase 2: Covid-19 Response (2020-2022)

#### Phase 2a: Market Stabilization (March-June 2020)

- SEK 300 billion asset purchase program announced
- Assets: government bonds, municipal bonds, covered bonds, commercial paper
- Enhanced weekly operations in kronor
- USD 60 billion dollar liquidity facility
- "Funding for lending" program (up to SEK 500 billion available)

**Purpose**: Restore market functioning during systemic stress.

**Outcome**: Market strains eased quickly; bid-ask spreads normalized within weeks. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

#### Phase 2b: Monetary Stimulus (June 2020-December 2021)

| Date | Envelope |
|------|----------|
| March 2020 | SEK 300 billion |
| June 2020 | SEK 500 billion |
| November 2020 | SEK 700 billion |

**Asset composition by end of 2021**:
- Covered bonds (largest share)
- Government bonds
- Municipal bonds
- Treasury bills
- Corporate bonds (SEK 10 billion)

(source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Phase 3: Inflation Response (2022-2024)

| Date | Policy Rate |
|------|------------|
| April 2022 | 0.25% (first hike) |
| September 2023 | 4.00% (peak) |
| May 2024 | 3.75% (first cut) |
| December 2024 | 2.50% |

**QE unwinding**:
- Asset purchases for reinvestment continued until January 2023
- Active bond sales (QT) began April 2023
- Government bond holdings fell from SEK 405 billion (May 2022) to SEK 100 billion (June 2025)

(source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Macroeconomic Effects

### Effects on Inflation

| Period    | GDP Effect    | Inflation Effect        | Source                        |
| --------- | ------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 2015-2019 | ~1% boost     | +20-50 basis points     | Kolasa, Laséen & Lindé (2025) |
| 2020-2023 | ~0.2% average | +0.25 percentage points | Akkaya et al (2023)           |

CPIF inflation returned to target by 2018-2019 after persistent undershooting. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Effects on Inflation Expectations

Survey-based inflation expectations at 2- and 5-year horizons:
- Displayed negative trend prior to 2015
- Reversed with introduction of unconventional policies
- Remained stable around 2% until early 2022

The evidence is consistent with unconventional policies successfully reanchoring expectations. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Effects on Interest Rates

Pass-through evidence (Berggren, Mammos & Strid 2024):
- Pass-through to interbank rate (STIBOR): ~1.0
- Pass-through to 3-month mortgage rate: ~1.0
- Pass-through to corporate loan rate: ~1.0
- Pass-through to deposit rate: substantial
- Pass-through to 10-year government bond: ~0.1

Interest rate spreads over policy rate remained relatively stable during unconventional policies, consistent with effective transmission. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Effects on Output

Estimates of conventional monetary policy effects in Sweden (1 percentage point policy rate increase):

| Study | Method | GDP Effect | Unemployment Effect |
|-------|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| Berggren et al (2024) | Bayesian VAR | -0.7% to -0.8% | +0.6 pp |
| Almerud et al (2024) | High-frequency + Proxy SVAR | -1.5% (peak) | increase |
| Coglianese et al (2025) | Narrative (2010-11 episode) | -5% | +2 pp |

All studies show effects peak with ~2 year delay. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Transmission Channels in Sweden

The Ravn-Wilkins evaluation emphasizes the **exchange rate channel** as particularly important for Sweden as a small, open economy:

1. **Exchange Rate Channel**: Kolasa, Laséen & Lindé (2025) find QE was effective largely because it led to real depreciation of the krona, stimulating exports and imported inflation

2. **Portfolio Balance Channel**: Standard mechanism; some evidence from Christensen & Zhang (2024)

3. **Signaling Channel**: Forward guidance was effective; Almerud et al (2024) find forward guidance shocks are close substitutes for actual rate changes

4. **Cash Flow Channel**: Particularly strong in Sweden given predominance of variable-rate mortgages (Flodén et al 2021)

(source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Negative Interest Rates in Sweden

### Implementation

- First negative rate: February 2015 (-0.10%)
- Lowest rate: -0.50% (February 2016)
- Duration at negative: February 2015 - December 2019

### Effectiveness

**Initial phase**: Strong pass-through to yields on liquid assets (money market rates, government debt)

**Diminishing returns**: Eggertsson et al (2024) find pass-through to lending rates declined below -0.25%, related to impact on bank equity values. This suggests the July 2015 and February 2016 cuts may have been ineffective through standard demand channels. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### "Reversal Rate" Evidence

Abadi, Brunnermeier & Koby (2023) theoretical work on the "reversal rate" - the point below which further cuts become counterproductive - is relevant to Sweden's experience. The evidence suggests Sweden may have approached this bound. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Balance Sheet Consequences

### Scale

| Date | Total Assets (% GDP) |
|------|---------------------|
| Pre-GFC | ~6% |
| 2009 peak | 22% |
| 2011 | 8.5% |
| Mid-2018 | 20% |
| Early 2022 (peak) | 27.5% |
| Mid-2025 | ~17% |

Note: Sweden's peak balance sheet (25% of GDP) was smaller than peers (ECB, Fed, BoE, BoJ). (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Maturity Mismatch

The Riksbank faced a stark maturity mismatch:
- **Assets**: Long-term bonds (government bonds, covered bonds)
- **Liabilities**: Short-term (overnight deposits, Riksbank Certificates)

When policy rates rose in 2022, this created large mark-to-market losses. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Capital Injection

In September 2024, the Swedish government provided a SEK 25 billion capital injection (0.39% of GDP).

The Riksbank had requested SEK 43.7 billion but received the smaller amount. Under the new Riksbank Act (2022:1568), equity should be at most SEK 60 billion (2023 prices, inflation-adjusted). (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Monetary-Fiscal Coordination Issues

### The Swedish Framework

Sweden operates under **strict monetary dominance**:
- Independent Riksbank pursuing 2% inflation target
- Fiscal rules: surplus target, expenditure ceiling, 35% debt anchor

This meant the burden of stabilization at the ZLB fell entirely on the Riksbank. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

### Lack of Debt Management Coordination

The National Debt Office continued issuing longer-term government bonds throughout the QE period, which the Riksbank then purchased. Alternative strategies were available:

1. **Joint monetary-fiscal expansion**: Relaxing fiscal rules temporarily
2. **Coordinated debt management**: NDO shortening debt maturity to share interest rate risk

Neither was pursued due to the strict institutional separation established after the 1992 crisis. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Swedish Wage-Setting and Inflation

A unique Swedish factor: the **Industrial Agreement** (Industriavtalet) from 1997 establishes coordinated wage bargaining:
- Manufacturing sector sets wage norm
- ~90% of employees covered by collective agreements
- Restrains wage-price spirals

During 2022-2023, wage increases were moderate (~3.7% annually) despite high inflation, contributing to real wage declines but helping anchor inflation expectations. This dampened the risk of second-round effects and reduced pressure on the Riksbank for even more aggressive tightening. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Key Findings from the 2026 Evaluation

### What Worked

1. Market stabilization during Covid-19 was highly effective
2. Negative rates and QE contributed to inflation returning to target in 2018-2019
3. Forward guidance was credible and effective
4. The wage-setting framework dampened inflation persistence

### What Didn't Work Well

1. Covid-era QE for monetary stimulus had modest effects (~0.2% GDP, ~0.25 pp inflation)
2. Corporate bond program was announced early but implemented late (after stress subsided)
3. Balance sheet risks were insufficiently assessed ex-ante
4. No coordination between QE and debt management strategy

### Recommendations

1. **Framework for unconventional tools**: Distinguish market-functioning purchases from monetary stimulus
2. **Exit strategies**: Set predefined principles for unwinding
3. **Monetary-fiscal coordination**: Enhance information sharing while respecting independence
4. **Balance sheet risk**: Improve assessment and communication
5. **Hold-to-maturity option**: Consider whether long-term government bonds should be valued at mark-to-market

(source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

## Comparison with Other Central Banks

| Feature | Sweden | ECB | Fed | BoE |
|---------|--------|-----|-----|-----|
| Negative rates | Yes (-0.50%) | Yes (-0.50%) | No | No |
| Peak balance sheet (% GDP) | 25% | ~60% | ~35% | ~45% |
| QT initiated | April 2023 | 2023 | 2022 | 2022 |
| Capital injection needed | Yes | No | No | No |

Sweden's smaller balance sheet (relative to GDP) reflects its smaller economy and the fact that the Riksbank purchased a more limited range of assets. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)
