# Forward Guidance

**Summary**: Forward guidance is a monetary policy tool in which central banks communicate about the future path of policy rates to influence current financial conditions and economic decisions.

**Research classification**: Both (theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence)

**Sources**: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018-unconventional-monetary-policies-in-the-euro-area-japan-and-the-united-kingd.md, bernanke-newtoolsmonetary-2020-pdf.md

**Last updated**: 2026-04-21

---

## Definition

Forward guidance provides market participants with information about the central bank's intentions for future policy rates. It works by shaping expectations of the future short-term rate path, which in turn affects current long-term rates, asset prices, and economic decisions. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Types of Forward Guidance

### Delphic vs. Odyssean

**Delphic guidance** (Campbell et al. 2012): Clarifies how monetary policy will evolve based on the central bank's economic forecasts. This is primarily informational - "here's what we expect to do given our outlook."

**Odyssean guidance**: Commits to keeping rates low even if economic conditions warrant tightening. This is potentially more powerful because it promises different behavior than a normal policy rule would imply. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Time-Based vs. State-Contingent

**Time-based**: Commits to a specific calendar date
- Example: "Rates will remain at current levels through at least 2014"

**State-contingent**: Ties policy to economic outcomes
- **Qualitative**: "until price stability is in sight" (Bank of Japan, 2010)
- **Quantitative**: "until unemployment falls below 7%" (Bank of England, 2013)

## Examples by Central Bank

### Federal Reserve

- **August 2011**: "exceptionally low levels for the federal funds rate at least through mid-2013"
- **January 2012**: Extended to "at least through late 2014"
- **December 2012**: Shifted to state-contingent: rates stay low until unemployment below 6.5% (provided inflation outlook remains below 2.5%)

### Bank of England

- **August 2013**: Rates stay low until unemployment falls below 7%
- **February 2014**: Updated guidance noting "considerable slack remains" despite approaching threshold

### Bank of Japan

- **October 2010**: Rates remain low until "price stability is in sight"
- **February 2012**: Rates stay low until inflation reaches "1 percent goal"
- **April 2013**: Continue until inflation exceeds 2% on sustained basis

### European Central Bank

- **July 2013**: "expects the key ECB interest rates to remain at present or lower levels for an extended period of time"

## Effectiveness

### Theoretical Benefits

1. Lower long-term rates via expectations channel
2. Stimulate current spending by reducing real rates
3. Reinforce [[quantitative-easing]] by clarifying policy intentions
4. Reduce interest rate uncertainty

### Challenges

**Anticipation problem**: If markets already expect rates to stay low, guidance adds nothing.

**Adverse signaling**: Guidance that rates will stay low for longer may signal pessimism about the economic outlook, potentially dampening confidence. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

**Credibility**: Time-inconsistency problem - when conditions improve, central bank has incentive to renege.

**ZLB trap** (Japan): When an economy is expected to remain at zero rates indefinitely, promises about distant future liftoff have limited impact on current decisions. (source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

## Interaction with QE

Forward guidance and QE are complementary:

1. **QE reinforces guidance**: Large bond holdings make early rate hikes costly, making low-rate commitment more credible
2. **Guidance clarifies QE intent**: Explaining the policy reaction function helps markets interpret asset purchases
3. **Both work through expectations**: Success depends on shaping beliefs about future policy

The ECB combined forward guidance with APP announcements: purchases would continue "until we see a sustained adjustment in the path of inflation." (source: ecbwp1956-pdf.md)

## Empirical Evidence

### UK Experience

Filardo and Hoffman (2014) found BoE forward guidance in 2013-2014:
- Did not reduce expected future rates
- Did not reduce government bond yields
- But reduced volatility of expected rates
- Likely helped keep rates low despite improving GDP growth

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Japan Experience

Forward guidance from 2010-2012 had muted effects because:
- Markets already expected rates to stay low
- BoJ credibility was damaged by past premature exits
- Deflationary expectations were deeply entrenched

(source: dell-ariccia-et-al-2018)

### Sweden Experience

Sweden stands out among peers for its long history of transparent forward guidance:

**Published Policy Rate Paths**: The Riksbank has published a policy rate path agreed by the Executive Board since 2007, making it a pioneer in this area. This is "Delphic" guidance - conditional on the economic forecast rather than Odyssean commitment. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

**Effectiveness**: Almerud et al (2024) find forward guidance in Sweden was highly effective, particularly for inflation outcomes. A forward guidance "shock" that increases the 2-year swap rate by one percentage point leads to:
- Gradual increase in policy rate peaking at 0.75 pp above normal level (~18 months later)
- Reduced inflation and output
- Exchange rate appreciation

Forward guidance announcements were found to be close substitutes for actual interest rate changes. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

**Why Sweden's Guidance Worked**:
1. Long track record (since 2007) conditioned markets to expect conditional forecasts
2. Clear communication that guidance is conditional, not a promise
3. Explicit emphasis that forecasts will change if outlook changes

This helped Sweden avoid the communication difficulties faced by other central banks (e.g., Reserve Bank of Australia's abrupt reversal in 2022). (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)

**Limitation - QE Guidance**: While interest rate guidance was effective, guidance around extraordinary tools (QE) was less flexible. Asset purchase programs during Covid were announced as fixed amounts over set horizons, rather than conditional on outlook. This reduced flexibility and sometimes "boxed in" the Riksbank - e.g., the corporate bond program was implemented in September 2020 even though market stress had already subsided. (source: riksbank-evaluation-12012026-pdf.md)
