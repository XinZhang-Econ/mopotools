This paper uses a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model with financial frictions to quantify the effects of the European Central Bank’s recent asset purchase programme. Our results suggest that the macroeconomic effects of purchases are potentially important when the programme is associated with forward guidance on the interest rate. The signalling that the future monetary policy stance will remain accommodative is the prominent transmission channel.

1. Introduction
In January 2015, the European Central Bank (ECB) announced the launch of an asset purchase programme (APP) with the objective to provide additional monetary policy stimulus in face of increasing deflation risks. It consists of purchases of private securities and euro-denominated investment-grade securities issued by euro area governments and institutions in the secondary market. Under this programme, the combined monthly purchases of public and private sector securities will amount to 60 billion euros and are intended to be carried out at least until end-September 2016. In March 2015, the first month of purchases under the APP, the amounts purchased were in line with the monthly target, comprising around 47 billion euro of sovereign bonds (with an average residual maturity of 8.6 years).

2. The framework
The framework is based on Gertler and Karadi (2013) and Cahn et al. (2014). The model economy is comprised of final good producers, intermediate goods producers, capital producers, households, a banking sector, employment agencies and the public sector (government and central bank). Households consume, save, and supply labour. They can hold private securities and long-term government bonds that are subject to transaction costs. Labour unions differentiate labour and sets wages in a monopolistically competitive market. Competitive labour packers buy labour services from the unions, pack it and sell it to intermediate goods firms. The goods market has a similar structure: retailers buy goods from intermediate goods firms, differentiate them and sell them in a monopolistically competitive market. The intermediate goods firms finance their capital acquisitions each period by obtaining funds from commercial banks. Capital producers purchase investment and depreciated capital to transform them into effective capital useable by intermediate goods firms for production. They face adjustment costs for investment. The aggregate final good is produced by perfectly competitive firms assembling a continuum of intermediate goods.
Banks transfer funds from households to firms, as well as to the government. 3. Model simulations
The effects of the APP are simulated in terms of monthly purchases and assets are assumed to be held to maturity (up to possibly 30 years). The quantity of central bank asset purchases are approximated with a second-order autoregressive process, with a cumulative total equalling approximately 9% of 2014 GDP.4 In our simulations, this purchase shock takes place in the first period, implying that economic agents know immediately the full path of the central bank portfolio and take their decisions in consequence.

Indeed, purchases of assets by the central bank frees up balance sheet capacity for investors to increase their purchases of alternative assets (portfolio rebalancing channel). This pushes up the prices of these assets, that increase by 3 percentage point the first year and 2 percentage point the second year. The degree of effectiveness of the APP depends clearly on its interactionwith a forward guidance policy. Forward guidance is an attempt tomanipulate the yield curve today bymaking statements about future monetary policy, i.e. announce a path of future interest rates. By committing to a future path of nominal interest rates, a central bank can ‘‘buy’’ current recovery, in ‘‘exchange for’’ temporary higher future inflation. This means that the period during which the interest rate will be blocked is important to generate significant effects. 4. Discussion
The results presented here are subject to various limitations. First, our main scenario assumed that only public bonds are purchased but the Eurosystem intends to allocate 90% of the total purchases to government bonds and 10% to securities issued by international organizations and multilateral development banks.8 Since purchases of private assets have a stronger macroeconomic impact (Fig. 4), our results might be slightly biased downwards. A second limitation comes from the deterministic nature of the simulations. In fact, there is plenty of shocks (such as those on oil prices) that can reduce the initial effects of purchases, especially on inflation. A third one relates to the assumption that all households have access to financial markets. Introducing hand-to-mouth consumers would reduce the effects of the APP. Another one is related to the possible announcement effect that takes place once the programme is announced and detailed and that can have already flattened the yield curve. And last, but not least, Del Negro et al. (2015) show that DSGE models tend to overestimate the impact of forward guidance, a phenomenon they call the ‘‘forward guidance puzzle’’. To solve this issue, they assume that agents face each period a constant probability of dying and being replace by a new agent, such that the aggregate economy discounts the future more heavily. Incorporating the same perpetual youth structure into our framework provides a dampening effect (Fig. 5): when the death probability is high (0.06), inflation and GDP growth are expected to increase by 30% less than in our central scenario. The signalling channel still remains the prominent transmission channel.
