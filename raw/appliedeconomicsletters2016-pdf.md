### Financial conditions and economic activity: the potential impact of the targeted long-term refinancing operations (TLTROs)

Financial conditions and economic activity: the potential impact of the targeted long-term refinancing operations (TLTROs) Hiona Balfoussia and Heather D. Gibson
Bank of Greece, Athens, Greece
ABSTRACT This article explores the relationship between financial conditions and real economic activity in the euro area as a whole and for Greece in particular. We use a financial conditions index (see Angelopoulou et al. 2014) which is constructed using a wide range of prices, quantities, spreads and survey data in line with theory. We update the indices and use them within a VAR framework to estimate the potential impact of the targeted long-term refinancing operations (TLTROs) on aspects of economic activity. Our results suggest that financial conditions do have a significant effect on economic activity, and thus the TLTROs, to the extent that they are designed to improve financial conditions, will provide a boost to the real economy.

I. Introduction
The importance of financial intermediation for real economic activity was first discussed by Gurley and Shaw (1955) and later first formalized in a macro model by Brainard and Tobin (1963). This early literature, which expanded rapidly, is nicely reviewed in Gertler (1988). During the 1990s, the academic debate further developed to include the credit chan-nel through the bank lending and balance sheet channels (see Bernanke and Gertler 1995; Bernanke, Gertler, and Gilchrist 1996, 1999), placing the emphasis on how financial market imperfections can amplify the effects of monetary policy through their impact on the supply of credit.
Tracking these theoretical developments, initial empirical papers examined the link between finance and the real economy focusing mainly on money and monetary policy in macroeconomic VAR mod-els (Christiano, Eichenbaum, and Evans 1996, 2005). They find that a contractionary monetary policy is associated with, inter alia, a persistent reduction in real GDP and retail sales. More recently, the litera-ture has broadened the set of variables beyond some monetary aggregate or the interest rate to also
include credit and share price indices (Fornari and Stracca 2013) as well as house prices, spreads between long and short rates, stock prices, loans-to-GDP and loans-to-deposits (Guarda and Jeanfils 2012). This broadening is supported by evidence that, since the 1970s, there has been a decoupling of money and credit and it is credit which has more explanatory power (Schularick and Taylor 2012).
However, the response of central banks to the international financial crisis of 2008 marked a move away from traditional monetary tools (such as the interest rate) to nonstandard measures, including quantitative easing and direct intervention in certain financial markets. In this context, measur-ing the stance of monetary policy requires a multi-faceted approach which moves beyond simply either the price of credit or its quantity.
In Angelopoulou, Balfoussia, and Gibson (2014), we construct a financial conditions index (FCI) using more than 20 variables, including not only interest rates but also spreads, quantities and survey data and show that it performs well in tracking financial conditions both pre- and post-crisis. In particular, we explore the potential impact of the ECB’s targeted long-term refinancing operations (TLTROs) on various measures of economic activity. Given its structure, we interpret the introduction of this nonstandard monetary policy measure as a posi-tive shock to credit to nonfinancial firms and house-holds and explore its impact on financial conditions in the euro area as a whole and in Greece in parti-cular and, through this channel, its potential impact on real activity. The empirical results provide sup-port for the hypothesis that financial conditions can indeed have a significant influence on economic activity.
II. The Eurosystem’s TLTRO programme – a point of departure
In June 2014, the ECB’s Governing Council decided to introduce a series of TLTROs, ‘designed to enhance the functioning of the monetary policy transmission mechanism by supporting bank lend-ing to the real economy’ (https://www.ecb.europa. eu/press/pr/date/2014/html/pr140605_2.en.html). In other words, they were designed to boost bank lending to euro-area nonfinancial corporations and households (mortgages excluded) by rewarding banks for extra credit provision with access to extra liquidity. According to the relevant press releases, under the scheme, banks would ‘initially be able to borrow an amount equivalent to up to 7% of a specific part of their loans in two operations in September and December 2014’ while, subsequently, additional amounts could be borrowed in further TLTROs, ‘depending on the evolution of the banks’ eligible lending activities in excess of bank-specific benchmarks’ (https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/ pr/date/2014/html/pr140703_2.en.html). Conversely, should they be found ex post to have performed worse than required in terms of their net lending to the private sector, participating banks would be obliged to repay the funds early.
The introduction of lending-contingent liquidity provision was a landmark decision for the
Eurosystem, marking a break from a long tradition of only implicit credit-supply targeting. The under-lying assumption was that an increase in credit has a short- and medium-term positive impact on real growth and thus on inflation, which was far below target. Taking this policy initiative as a starting point, we attempt to explore the possible effect of credit supply and, more generally, financial condi-tions on real activity.
III. Methodology
We follow a stepwise procedure. We build on the FCIs of Angelopoulou, Balfoussia, and Gibson (2014), which summarize and track the evolution of financial conditions over time. These are con-structed by applying principal components analysis on a wide range of prices, quantities, spreads and survey data, in a way that incorporates insights from the theoretical literature and focuses on supply-side aspects of financial conditions, thus rendering them well-suited for the present exploration.
We first update the FCIs to April 2014, the end date of the TLTRO benchmark period (see Fig. 1). We then make the working assumption that the full amount of liquidity allotted to euro area banks dur-ing the initial two TLTRO rounds (i.e. 7% of their loans to the euro area nonfinancial private sector outstanding on 30 April 2014) is indeed directly channelled towards loans to the private sector.1
This positive one-off shock to credit supply is then inputted into the corresponding FCI components, using the loadings on loan flows to nonfinancial corporations and households in each of the principle components and the weight of each principle com-ponent in the FCI. In this way, the implied positive shock to each of the FCIs, i.e. to financial conditions, is thus calculated.
Subsequently we estimate alternative VAR model specifications, each of which includes a measure of real activity, the FCI and, for completeness, as exter-nal variables, inflation and the ‘fiscal news’ variable of Gibson, Hall, and Tavlas (2012), appropriately updated for the purposes of this article.2 We then

IV. Results
Figure 1 presents the updated FCIs; an increase in the index reflects an easing of financial conditions and zero corresponds to a long-term mean level. Their evolution over recent years is interesting in itself. While in the euro area the great troughs in financial conditions, corresponding to the Lehman Brothers crisis and to the European sovereign debt crisis, were roughly of equal magnitude, for Greece (as for many of the other peripheral countries), the sovereign debt crisis was, literally, much closer to home, as reflected in the far sharper decline of the FCI in the second trough.

whether they contribute positively to the adjusted R2.
Turning now to our main question of interest which is to gauge the potential impact on the real economy of a positive change in financing condi-tions, stemming from an increase in credit supply, Table 3 presents the relevant Granger causality tests. They indicate Granger causality running from the FCI to the real variables in all specifications, while no Granger causality is detected in the opposite direction in all cases but one. This is interesting, given that the main caveat of this type of analysis is that bidirectional causality is typically hard to reject.
The estimated effect of the ongoing TLTRO programme on credit supply to the private sector and, ultimately, on real activity is depicted in Figs 2–7 which present the cumulative impulse responses of the three real variables to the change in financial conditions implied by the TLTRO shock. On the whole, the real variables’ impulse responses to the TLTRO shock on the FCI are positive and significant for the euro area and for Greece. They indicate that, were the entire initial TLTRO allocation of the Eurosystem to be instan-taneously channelled towards lending to the pri-vate sector, the real effects would be sizeable, namely a 5.7%, 2.9% and 4.7% cumulative increase of IP, RRT and the PMI for the euro area at the 4-year horizon and a 0.9%, 6.6% and 2.9% cumula-tive increase of the corresponding variables in the case of Greece. It is notable that, for the euro area, the effect on IP is quite sizable, possibly implying long-lasting benefits stemming from the accumula-tion of productive capital. Conversely, in the case of Greece, the effect on IP is small and insignif-icant, reflecting the weak industrial base of the Greek economy as compared to the euro area average. The potential impact of a credit increase on RRT seems to be much greater for Greece than for the euro area as a whole, capturing the more consumption-driven composition of the Greek economy relative to the euro area. The effect on the PMI is significant and substantial in both cases. While these results are intuitive and consistent across estimations, some care is needed in interpret-ing them. Firstly, as already mentioned, TLTROs actually ‘reward’ not only a net increase in lending, but also a decline in deleveraging, i.e. an
improvement vis-à-vis the previously established trend. Thus, the estimated impact captured by the impulse response functions should be thought of as a deviation from an existing trend, as a relative improvement over an otherwise more adverse trajec-tory, rather than as an end figure. Moreover, in prac-tice, any increase in new lending would take place over a number of months, rather than immediately, implying a slower realization of this same cumulative impact, while the degree of bank participation in the programme is also a factor which would affect its success. On the other hand, the estimates might also be biased downwards, as any policy initiative which leads to an increase in net lending would also be likely to positively affect other variables within the FCI. Thus, via the ‘money multiplier’ effect, the end result could be higher than indicated by the above estimates.
