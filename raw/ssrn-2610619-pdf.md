Abstract
Healthcare services are complex and dynamic in nature, and have been the focus of several
business improvement efforts. While various techniques in the literature have attempted process
improvement, process redesign efforts largely use assumed “as-is” models instead of real
process executions. In this paper, we propose an evidence based approach for business process
redesign, making use of process execution data. We compare various clustering algorithms for
process mining and demonstrate usefulness of clustering. Subsequently, we propose using
simulation along with data envelopment analysis at a process level to find the most efficient
processes, and to evaluate multiple what-if scenarios.
Keywords: Business Process Re-Design; Process Improvement; Process Mining; Simulation;
Data Envelopment Analysis; Evidence Based Redesign.
1. Introduction
In the wake of healthcare reforms, most healthcare organizations are focusing on ways to
streamline their processes, with an increased emphasis on usage of information systems
(Anyanwu et al, 2003). Delivering a high quality service is non-negotiable, while reducing costs
simultaneously (Anyanwu et al, 2003; Mans et al, 2009). The above challenges can be addressed
to a certain extent by managing and redesigning business processes to achieve cost and time
efficiency (Gottlieb et al, 2010; Rohner, 2012).
One of the earliest and most cited works in the redesign domain, Hammer and Champy (1993),
define reengineering as “the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to
achieve dramatic improvements in critical contemporary measures of performance, such as cost,
quality, service, and speed.” This radical approach for process improvements is also supported
by Al-Mashari et al (2000). Davenport (1993), another pioneer, defines process redesign as “the
analysis and design of workflows and processes within and between organizations. Business
activities…should be broken down into processes that can be designed for maximum
effectiveness...”. There have been several other definitions of BPR over the years. Habib et al
(2011, 2013) have congregated them and state that while Talwar (1993) focuses on
organizational structure and value chain, Petrozzo and Stepper (1994) indicate operational
improvements. Goksoy et al (2012), voted for more moderate changes and transitions for a firm’s
survival. Hence, looking at the multitude of definitions and approaches, we can conclude that
despite varying frameworks, approaches and objectives, all the researchers unanimously support
comprehensive improvements when it comes to BPR (Habib, 2011).
However, a radical approach as outlined by BPR might not be appropriate for all business
domains (Becker et al, 2007). Healthcare, which is commonly known to be an extremely
complex environment (Poulymenopoulou et al, 2003), poses such an issue. As Becker et al
(2007) argue, with multiple regulations, legal frameworks and medical guidelines to be followed,
healthcare becomes a very delicate domain where radical changes might upset the system instead
of improving it (The Medical Letter Inc, 2004; 2006). Becker et al (2007) and Van Der Aalst et
al (2003) propose the concept of business process management (BPM), which calls for gradual
changes, offering continuous improvements for process optimization. As per Becker et al (2007),
BPM offers a way of process improvement which works within the given framework and
organizational structures. Thus for the purpose of our research, when we talk about process
redesign, we refer to the consistent, iterative improvement which BPM refers to.
Zellner (2011) provides an overview of business process improvement approaches, and
concludes that there is a gap in the support for process improvement. As per the author, “Most
approaches concentrate on what needs to be done before and after the improvement act, but the
act of improving itself still seems to be a black box”. According to Forster et al (2006), “the act
of improving processes” is the most value adding phase in any BPM project which has poor
support of specific guidelines. On similar lines, Vergidis et al (2006) also state that although
there have been several attempts towards establishing BPR frameworks, there is a gap wherein
process optimization is concerned. Apart from the above specific arguments, Hagemeyer et al
(2006), Siha et al (2008), and Habib et al (2013) also give a structured and comprehensive
overview of redesign approaches; develop conceptual frameworks or a classification scheme for
problem solving tools.
In general, there are two different categories of techniques which are used in the redesign of
business processes: one is more qualitative and the other is quantitative in its nature and
application (Hee et al, 2000). Qualitative techniques focus on heuristics and best practices, as
outlined by MIT Process Handbook (2003), Netjes and Mansar (2005), Zellner (2011) etc. These
aim to apply the approach of “best practices” for process analysis and improvement. A best
practice is a historical solution that seems worthwhile to replicate in another situation or setting
(Netjes et al, 2009). Approaches which apply case based reasoning also fall into this category,
namely Ku et al (1996) and Min et al (1996).
Quantitative techniques include simulation and analytical/algorithmic approaches. Hee et al
(2000) argues that simulation can be seen as an approximation technique. However, its use has
been well documented when it comes to business process re-design (Hlupic et al, 1998; Maruster
et al, 2009; Liu et al, 2012), and several more which will be discussed in the subsequent sections.
Studies spanning such a long timeline and in various domains establish without a question the
validity of simulation as a useful approach towards BPR. Algorithmic approaches on the other
hand apply mathematical theories to analyse business processes, like markov chains, queuing
theory, coloured petri nets, genetic algorithms etc. (Hofacker et al, 2001; Tijms et al, 1994, Van
Der Aalst et al, 1996).
Furey (1993) suggests the following methodology for implementing BPR:
 Map (measure, model, define, flowchart) the existing process
 Identify the customer driven objectives / business objectives for the process
 Analyse and modify the existing process
 Look for different and best practice examples
 Re-engineer the process to reach a final version
 Roll out the process, using pilot studies and simulations; and subsequently implement it
in totality.
Coupled with previous initiatives and the above steps of process redesign, we can notice that the
studies in literature have primarily two limitations. The first is that for any redesign project, the
starting point always is specified, for example, implementation of information system in a
hospital, or reduction in length of stay. This can possibly limit the scope of the redesign project,
restricting the process analyst’s view on other steps to reduce bottlenecks.
A second limitation is the type of “as-is” process models being used as the starting point. All the
popular redesign approaches make use of assumed process models as the starting point, focusing
on traditional process discovery mechanisms like interviewing business users and conducting
workshops with process stakeholders. Ironically, a wealth of information in the form of process
execution logs lies dormant and unused. It would be more prudent to make use of the immense
information available to us in the form of process traces; since traditional decision making
methods do not make use of the humongous volumes of data to make decisions which can be
justified.
In this research, we present a framework for evidence based redesign of business processes in
healthcare domain. The approach makes use of the techniques of process mining, simulation
and data envelopment analysis in an integrated manner, to present a decision support system
for process redesign. We propose a step by step methodology for redesign by processing actual
event log from hospital information systems, which is unstructured in nature.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we present a literature review
relevant to the issues addressed in this paper. Section 3 outlines our proposed framework for the
research in detail along with preliminary results. Section 4 outlines our data and tool support.
Section 5 lists the contributions and managerial implications of this research.
2. Related Work
2.1 Process Discovery – Unearthing “as-is” process model
The starting point of any re-design project in an organization has to be the modelling of existing
processes. As also supported by Davenport (1993), Hammer and Champy (1993) and Datta
(1998), as-is models are an unavoidable and mandatory requirement in any BPR project; so as to
help stakeholders grasp the existing organizational structure and the redesigning that needs to be
carried out. Having said that, they also recognize that extracting as-is models is a non-trivial task
and incurs huge costs to the organizations. The same literature also argues that the existing
procedures for as-is model extraction are ad-hoc. They can involve several man-hours of
discussions and workshops; yet can lack rigour as different consultants can come up with
different as-is models for the same organization. The same viewpoints are later supported by
other authors, particularly Van Der Aalst et al (2003), Lang et al (2008) who state the traditional
process discovery is time consuming. Van Der Aalst et al (2003) and Verner (2004) also identify
the issue of discrepancies between actual and described business processes.
Processminingoffersusaninterestingapproachtomitigatetheaboveproblems(VanDerAalst
et al, 2007). One of the earliest approaches to automatic process discovery was of Datta (1998),
who postulated algorithms for automatic extraction of as-is models from “real-life processes”.
Although not termed as process mining, this approach utilized the concepts of process modelling
and grammar discovery. Cook and Wolf were also among the pioneers of process mining. They
introduced process discovery in the field of software development projects based on produced
event logs (Cook and Wolf, 1998).  Simultaneous work was also done on process discovery by
Agarwal et al (1998).
Conceptually, process mining takes a process perspective for analysing event log data (Gunther,
2009). Weijters et al (2007), Medeiros et al (2004, 2008), Song et al (2008) and Rozinat et al
(2009) describe process mining techniques as an attempt to extract non-trivial and useful
information from event logs. Process mining algorithms can extract process knowledge from
process event logs (an event log refers to a set of process instances), without any a-priori
knowledge. However, information systems in most healthcare organizations provide us with
unstructured event logs of process executions. Unstructured event logs refer to a set of loosely
defined process instances which offer runtime flexibility; hence there is no strict execution path.
When such logs are mined using generic process mining algorithms, they tend to reveal
“spaghetti models” which are hard to comprehend.
Process mining has shown its applicability in numerous areas especially health care (Van Der
Aalst et al, 2003; Maruster et al, 2005; Gupta et al, 2007; Mans et al, 2008; Lang et al, 2008;
Quaglini et al, 2009), fraud detection (Yang et al, 2006; Jans et al, 2008) and testing of wafer
scanners (Rozinat et al, 2009). For the purpose of BPR, process mining has been used only
recently, by Aguirre et al (2013), Maruster et al (2009) and Van Beest et al (2007).
The more unstructured a process is, the more opportunity it provides for process mining to
deliver interesting results. As per Gunther, (2009), process mining techniques when applied to
well defined processes, reveal only the process as it had been intended to work. But when we
deal with flexible processes which might exhibit diverse behaviour, process mining reveals much
more interesting results and insights. Such processes also yield “spaghetti models” (Greco et al,
2004; Hammori et al, 2006; Song et al, 2009; Van Der Aalst et al, 2007), which are difficult to
comprehend. Thus, flexible environments like healthcare should be seen as a “two-sided sword”
for process mining opportunities: offering the most value out of the technique while at the same
time requiring extra insights and expertise to decipher the said value.

2.2 Business Process Analysis – Using Simulation and DEA
Although process mining automates the data collection and the model construction, it only
provides users with an as-is model. The aim of modeling any business process is to be able to
improve it. Although this can be used as a basis for process redesign, it fails to enable managers
to evaluate alternative solutions. Bozkaya et al (2009), Zhou and Piramuthu (2010) provide
frameworks for process improvement using process mining; but do not provide any methodology
to do so. One step towards fully automated decision support systems is to combine process
mining with operation research techniques, which has also been identified in the Process Mining
Manifesto (IEEE, 2011) as one of the challenges which need to be addressed in the process
mining domain.
When more than one redesign alternatives are proposed, it is essential to evaluate them against
each other. As per Doomun et al (2008), simulation analysis can be an extremely useful approach
for this evaluation. As cited in Doomun et al (2008), “A BPR methodology that concludes to a
continuous improvement model is very strong. ….process improvement becomes an every day
task and both radical redesign and continuous process improvement becomes part of processes'
lifecycle”. Supported strongly by literature, we use the approach of simulation of business
processes (Brailsford et al, 2009; Carr and Roberts, 2010; Gunal and Pidd, 2011) to mimic our
mined business processes accurately. We could find only a handful of studies which combine
process mining with simulation in the literature: Maruster (2009), Rozinat et al (2009), Liu et al
(2012), Mans et al (2013),.
As we have already discussed, traditional process discovery mechanisms are too time-consuming
and expensive for an organization to carry out, and do not always offer a rigorous and correct
picture of the process flow. On the other hand, process mining affords us an avenue for creating
process models which reflect reality as close as possible, thus making future redesign initiatives
also more rigorous, viable and trustworthy. Thus, process mining is used to discover “as-is”
processes and simulation can be used to discover and evaluate “to-be” processes.
2.2.1 Simulation
Dohmen et al (2010) states that predicting the consequence of any changes in business processes
is usually difficult. Especially in scenarios such as health care, it becomes all the more difficult
since implementation of various to-be processes is neither possible nor feasible. Hence,
simulation can be used to reproduce different scenarios and thus enable a “what-if” analysis
(Bratley et al, 1987), Banks et al (1998).
Doomun et al (2008) state that in operational research, simulation of business processes is one of
the most popular applications. Swami (1995) also cites that for BPR projects, use of simulation is
recommended. Gunal and Pidd (2010) undertake a comprehensive survey of simulation
modelling in healthcare; and classify papers according to the areas of their application. They
classify simulation in healthcare literature into Accident and Emergency (A&E) units, inpatient
facilities, outpatient clinics, other hospital units and whole hospital simulations. Another seminal
paper by Fone et al, (2003), concluded earlier that most of the simulation applications in
healthcare aim at improving decision making and planning; thus effectively aiming at process
redesign through simulation. A comprehensive review by Jun (1999) is also often cited. This
paper mentions previously conducted research on healthcare clinics, analysing patient flows and
resource allocation.
Bertolini et al (2011) also carries out a BPR effort in a surgical ward in a hospital using
simulation and event process chains. The authors in their structured literature review cite several
studies by Blake et al (1991), Barnes and Quiason (1997), Sanchez et al (2000), Ratcliffe et al
(2001), Dexter and Traub (2002), Bowers and Mould (2004), who use simulation models to
analyse and improve healthcare processes in various departments. Most recently, Al-Refaie et al
(2014) also applied simulation to improve performance of an emergency department in Jordanian
hospital. As cited in the paper, simulation analysis has also been used by Weng et al (2011) for
resource allocation, Meng and Spedding (2008) for reduction in patient waiting times, and by
Cabrera et al (2011) to design a decision support system for emergency department.
With such immense support from literature, we believe that the combination of automatic
process discovery and simulation capabilities offer an innovative way to analyse and redesign
business processes. The discovered process models inevitably describe reality better than most
hand-crafted simulation models. Our approach contrasts markedly with traditional approaches,
which use assumed “as-is” process models. Majority of the existing approaches assume that
simulation experiments are run from an empty initial state. We however populate our simulation
model with real data from process executions, available to us in the form of event logs. In the
next sub-section we look at how we propose to compare various what-if scenarios resulting out
of simulation, using data envelopment analysis.
2.2.2 Data Envelopment Analysis
Subsequent to developing a simulation model, we want to rank the various what-if scenarios in
terms of their efficiency and other pre-decided performance indicators. For complex processes
such as those in healthcare (Weng et al, 2011), using DEA is advantageous.
Farrell (1957) was the first to work on relative efficiency analysis. His work was formulated as a
linear programming problem by Charnes et al (1978) who proposed the methodology of data
envelopment analysis. Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is used to measure the efficiency of
decision making units (DMUs) based on a limited set of assumptions (Charnes et al, 1978).
According to Copper et al, (2004), and Weng et al (2011), DEA is extremely useful in cases
where the production function is not known, i.e. the relationship between multiple inputs and
multiple outputs is not specified. Grosskopf and Valdmanis (1987), Wilson and Jadlow (1982),
Nunamaker (1983) and Sherman (1984) have also implemented DEA in healthcare efficiency
measurements. According to Lovell (1993), efficiency measurement techniques can be classified
into parametric and non-parametric methods, with DEA falling in the latter category.
Dohmen et al (2010) and simultaneously Burger et al (2010) were a few studies which applied
DEA for the measurement of business process efficiency. Prior to this, DEA had been applied
but at an organizational level, like in banks by Berger et al (1997), Thanassoulis (1999), Cinca et
al (2001) and Goddard et al (2001); airline networks (Adler and Golany, 2001) and purchasing
departments (Murphy et al., 1996).
Weng et al (2008) succinctly put together the advantages of using DEA. The ability of DEA to
handle multiple inputs and outputs in the absence of a functional relation is probably the
strongest advantage, and one which is also supported by Sexton (1986) and Thanassoulis (1993).
Further, the possibility that one or more peers can be compared against is another notable
advantage which encourages the use of DEA over other approaches (George, 2005). In the next
section we outline our framework, and will also look at simulation and DEA methodology in
more detail.

3.1 Process Discovery Using Process Mining
Clustering algorithms for mining process models have become increasingly popular. Several
researchers through the years such as Greco et al (2004) (k-means algorithm), Medeiros et al
(2008) who propose hierarchical clustering, Jung et al (2008) who use cosine similarity measure
for hierarchical clustering, Song et al (2008) who implement self organizing maps, have worked
on clustering workflow traces. Sequence clustering was recently implemented by Ferreira et al
(2012). In the subsequent section, we will compare a few clustering algorithms for process
mining and zero in on the most appropriate algorithm for our purpose, for clustering flexible and
unstructured processes, for process discovery.
3.1.1 Clustering Algorithms for Process Discovery
When process models are discovered from unstructured event logs, general algorithms tend to
produce spaghetti models, not including an appropriate level of abstraction. Numerous studies
illustrate the above difficulty (Gunther et al, 2009; Bose et al, 2009; Veiga et al, 2010;
Goedertier et al, 2011). Clustering techniques can be considered a feasible solution for
simplifying event logs and reducing the complexity of process models. The original event log
can be divided into various sub-logs which can then be further analysed to reveal simpler process
models; hence this aids in increasing comprehensibility (Weerdt et al, 2013).
Disjunctive Workflow Schema was implemented in ProM as the DWS Plugin. In this algorithm,
the complete log is examined and a model is generated using the heuristic miner (Weijters et al,
2006). Then the log is compared to the model to measure the model quality. If the generated
model is optimal, the algorithm converges. Otherwise, the log is further split using the k-means
clustering and the resulting models are further tested. If there is still over-generalization, further
splitting takes place till the algorithm achieves optimal models. However, applying the algorithm
on our event log resulted in many such non-allowed for behaviors, and hence it is not considered
for process discovery.
Trace Clustering is another approach for process discovery, implemented by Greco et al (2004)
and then Song et al (2008). The motivation behind this work was the existence of flexible
environments such as healthcare, where processes do not follow a certain path, and human
intervention is common which changes the execution of each case (Veiga et al, 2010). A set of
items describes a case from a particular perspective which forms a profile. An item is a metric
which assigns a numeric value to each case, and hence each profile can be looked at as a vector.
These vectors are then used to calculate the distance between cases, using any distance measure
like Euclidean or Jaccard or Hamming distance. Then any of the clustering methods like k-means
or self-organizing maps (SOM) can be used to group cases in the same cluster.
Like the techniques described above, sequence clustering can take a set of sequences and group
them into clusters, so that similar types of cases are in the same cluster (Ferreira et al, 2012). But
sequence clustering works directly with the sequences, and not on features derived from them.
This algorithm has also been used extensively in bioinformatics, to classify large protein datasets
into different families (Enright et al, 2000). However, sequence clustering also results in
different clusters over multiple runs, hence not converging or taking a very long time to reach an
optimal solution.
Gunther and Van Der Aalst (2005) proposed the fuzzy miner for process discovery. This
technique allows for simplifying process models by highlighting important flows. This however
can prove to be a major drawback for the scope of our research. This algorithm removes process
variants and exceptions. Our aim of process mining is to discover all variants, outliers and
exceptions in various clusters, and the fuzzy miner does not serve our purpose in this case.
The trace clustering algorithm developed by Song et al (2008) scores above Greco et al (2004) in
the generation of profiles from several perspectives, such as control flow, organization, data etc.
Hence, we can derive clusters based on not only activities and transitions, but also other
parameters listed above. We can also influence the attributes used for clustering which makes the
results more intuitive. Hence, for the purpose of our research, we use the trace clustering
algorithm, for process discovery and cluster creation.

3.1.2 Preliminary Results
As discussed above, we use the trace clustering algorithm implemented in the ProM framework,
by Song et al (2008). Every clustering algorithm works towards grouping sets of similar points
together. For trace clustering, those relevant points are traces.
The algorithm proceeds by “profiling an event log” (Song et al, 2008) – “measuring a set of
traces with a number of profiles to form an aggregate vector”. Then these vectors can be used to
calculate the distance between the traces. Song et al (2008) describe the various kinds of profiles
which are component of the trace clustering algorithm, which we summarise below.
In the “activity profile”, for each type of activity (event name) an item is defined. Measuring an
item is done by counting all the events which have the same activity name, thus constituting the
activity profile of the event log.
In the “originator profile”, items are considered as originators in the event log. It counts how
many events have originated from that particular item, per  trace. It is to be noted that this metric
also belongs to the organizational miner, where it provides a flat model without any clustering.
In the “transition profile”, an item measures how many times an event is followed by another,
thus measuring effectively an “is-proceeded-by” kind of relation between two activities.
“Case attributes” works best where there is more information embedded in the event log; the data
of the cases can be compared with each other. As per our understanding, this profiling will be the
most useful in cases where context information is available along with the event log.
In “event attributes”, the items are the data attributes of all the events in the log. Thus, like case
attributes, this profile works when there is information embedded in the events of the traces.
“Performance attribute” is markedly different from other profiles. There are a predefined set of
items; which in the presence of a timestamp can measure case duration, minimum, maximum and
mean time difference between events for each trace. Since the ultimate aim of our research is the
operational excellence of processes; we use this profile in our paper for demonstration purposes.
Clustering techniques can use several measures to calculate similarity between cases, like
Euclidean, Jaccard, Hamming distance etc. Clustering algorithms which can be used are also
numerous: K-means, quality threshold, agglomerative hierarchical clustering, self organizing
maps (SOM) etc. In their paper, Song et al (2008) conclude that the combination of Euclidean
distance with SOM performs better than others, when being clustered by “activity profile”.
However, when we use the “performance profile” and the combination of Euclidean distance
with SOM algorithm, we get clusters which demonstrate spaghettiness again. Also, the algorithm

4. Data Description and Tool Support
We make use of the raw data of a gynecological oncology department in the AMC Hospital, a
large academic hospital in Netherlands. This data has been published by the IEEE for the BPI
challenge in 2011. The event log contains traces for 1143 patients over a period of three years
(from 2005 to 2008); and contains 150291 events. The process for gynecological oncology
patients is supported by several different departments, e.g. gynecology, radiology and several
labs. It should be noted that the log contains 376 different event names which indicate the
complexity of the concerned process.
In order to perform process mining, ProM is used. ProM is a process mining tool which
implemented the heuristics approach (Van der Aalst et al, 2007). The ProM tool is a combination
of many other tools, all using the same type of event log. ProM is able to cover a wide range of
plug-ins offering functionalities such as process mining, export, import, analysis, and conversion.
The tool is developed at Eindhoven University of Technology and has been used in various case
studies as well as to analyse health care processes (Van der Aalst et al, 2007. For these reasons,
the ProM tool is applied in the research.
5. Further Work: Process Re-Design / Optimization
When our process models are satisfactorily simulated with validation, we undertake the redesign
aspect of the research by outlining KPIs. Undertaking an iterative approach is the most
acceptable and the least disruptive option. It is to be noted that the data available to us is
secondary in nature and hence we will choose KPIs without any expert assistance or vast domain
knowledge. We divide the redesign initiative of the research into “stages”.
In stage 1, the basic conditions for the analysis have to be defined. As a starting point, we set a
goal of efficiency like reduction in cycle time, cost or some other parameter. Output variables
can be process instances or a performance indicator for the process. Depending on the chosen
input and output variables, we can outline various what-if scenarios. These will essentially
reflect the changes that will take place in the business processes.
In stage 2, for a given business process, DEA can be applied to measure the efficiency of process
instances and subsequently of the process by using the DEA approach proposed by Burger et al
(2010). As DEA can apply a multiple input-output model, the efficiency of a process can be
measured by integrating all performance indicators defined in stage 1.
Possible options for changes could be modifications of the process model, the reallocation of
staff or the sequence of process instances. Having simulated the “what if” scenarios results can
be compared and evaluated by again using DEA.
The scenario chosen in the last step of stage 2 is again analyzed by using BPS to identify further
opportunities of efficiency improvement in terms of the previously defined input and output
variables. Again the options for changes being available now, can be applied in simulation
scenarios. This leads to new simulation results which are then again compared by using DEA.
This procedure is continued (stage n) until the efficiency of the process reaches a satisfactory or
predefined level. Since the external variables might also change, like policies etc., this analysis
can be seen as a continuous improvement procedure.
This approach combines the advantage of using DEA for analyzing input-output relations, and
simulation for further examination. Another major advantage is that unlike one time redesign
efforts, process efficiency is being measured on a continuous basis. Whenever DEA is applied
here, we use the same input-output variables defined in the first stage. This is necessary to ensure
that efficiency measurements stay comparable throughout stages. Using only simulation, it is not
possible to rank the simulation scenarios in terms of process efficiency (Braglia et al, 1999). In
our proposed method, all scenarios which can be simulated in every phase can be compared with
each other, leading us to an iterative approach of process redesign. However, the input output
variables need to be chosen carefully at stage 1; since for each set of input output variables, a
different iteration of DEA and simulation starting from stage 1 will need to be run.
5. Discussion and Managerial Implications
In comparison with state of the art for process mining, previous process mining research has
primarily focused on process discovery. Our research however, focuses on covering a majority
of the BPM lifecycle – process discovery, process analysis as well as process redesign. In this
particular paper, we carry out a successful implementation of a part of the framework as proof of
concept. This result also helps in establishing the feasibility of the proposed framework.
The proposed framework emphasizes the interconnection of various methodologies, well
established in their domains, to aid towards an analytical and evidence based method of process
improvement. In this research, we address the challenges related to process mining on real world
healthcare data. We target issues in majority of the process lifecycle, from the preliminary steps
of process discovery to techniques for process analysis and subsequent redesign. We also aim to
address a research challenge raised by IEEE in the “Process Mining Manifesto (2011)” – that of
combining process mining with other analysis techniques from various domains.
The most important contribution is perhaps that we expect to bridge academic process mining
research with real world healthcare systems. Healthcare processes have been commonly
described as dynamic, adhoc, unstructured; and hence an area for process redesign with great
potential, but very little work has described actual data-based process redesign.
The proposed framework aims at providing both a business foundation for evidence based
process redesign, as well as a clear guidance for future redesign projects in even complex and
delicate settings such as healthcare.
